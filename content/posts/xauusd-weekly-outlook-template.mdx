A weekly outlook is only useful if it's specific enough to be wrong. "Gold could go either way" isn't analysis — it's a hedge against being held accountable. This is a template for building a real weekly outlook: one with a stated bias, a level that invalidates it, and the structure that got you there.

Use it as a repeatable format. Swap in real levels and structure notes each week rather than starting from a blank page.

## Start with higher timeframe structure

Before touching a single lower timeframe chart, pull up the weekly and daily. Note the most recent higher-timeframe break of structure and whether price is currently trading in a **premium** or **discount** zone relative to the latest swing range.

![Weekly XAU/USD candles with the upper half of the range shaded as premium and the lower half shaded as discount, split by the equilibrium line](/images/xauusd-weekly-hero.png)

Premium and discount aren't exotic concepts — they're just the top and bottom halves of the most recent meaningful swing range, split at the 50% level (equilibrium). If price is in the upper half (premium) of an uptrend's range, you're less interested in chasing longs and more interested in what happens if price pulls back into discount first. The reverse applies in a downtrend.

## Key levels to watch

Every outlook should name specific, checkable levels — not vague zones:

- **Previous week's high and low.** These are the most obvious liquidity draws going into a new week, and price frequently reaches for one before establishing real direction.
- **Untapped fair value gaps.** Gaps left behind by displacement moves that haven't been revisited often act as magnets, especially on the daily timeframe.
- **Unmitigated daily order blocks.** If the most recent leg up or down left a clean order block that price hasn't returned to, that's a level worth having on the chart — see our [guide to identifying valid order blocks](/blog/understanding-order-blocks-ict) for how to filter these properly.

Write the actual numbers down. "Around the recent high" is not a level. "2,487 — the August 12th swing high" is a level you can be proven wrong against.

## Read the market structure, not just the price

A break of structure on the daily chart changes the entire outlook, even if price is only a few dollars from where it was the day before. Ask specifically:

1. Is the most recent leg a continuation of the existing trend, or a change of character signaling exhaustion?
2. Did the last pullback hold higher (or lower) than the prior one, confirming the trend is intact?
3. Is there a liquidity pool sitting just beyond the current range that the market is likely to reach for before reversing?

Gold in particular tends to sweep obvious highs and lows — equal highs, equal lows, round numbers — before making its real move. Treat an initial break of an obvious level with more suspicion than a break of a level nobody was watching.

## State a bias — and an invalidation

This is the step most outlooks skip, and it's the one that actually matters:

> State a clear directional bias for the week and the specific level that would invalidate it. Avoid vague "could go either way" calls — the value of an outlook is in taking a position and being accountable to it.

A complete bias statement looks like: "Bias is bullish above 2,470 while price holds above the daily order block; a daily close below that level shifts bias to neutral/bearish and the previous range low becomes the next liquidity target." That's a call you can review on Friday and actually learn from — not a paragraph vague enough to be "right" no matter what happens.

## Putting it together: a repeatable checklist

- [ ] Weekly and daily trend direction identified
- [ ] Current price located in premium or discount of the active range
- [ ] Previous week's high/low marked
- [ ] Untapped fair value gaps and unmitigated order blocks marked
- [ ] Most recent structure shift (or lack of one) noted
- [ ] Bias stated with a specific invalidation level
- [ ] Key liquidity target identified if bias plays out

Run through this same list every week. The value isn't in any single call being right — it's in building a track record you can actually review, refine, and learn from over time.
